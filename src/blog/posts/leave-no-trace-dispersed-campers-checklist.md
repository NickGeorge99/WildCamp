## Why Leave No Trace Matters for Dispersed Campers

Leave No Trace is not just a nice idea. It is the reason dispersed camping on public land is still allowed.

Every year, land managers consider closing areas to camping because of trash, illegal fire rings, human waste, and damaged vegetation left behind by careless visitors. When enough people trash a spot, the BLM or Forest Service restricts access for everyone. The campsite you love today could be closed tomorrow because someone before you did not clean up.

Dispersed camping comes with a deal: you get to camp for free on public land, and in return, you leave it exactly as you found it — or better. The seven Leave No Trace principles provide the framework, but this checklist translates them into specific, actionable steps for dispersed campers.

## Before You Leave Home

### Plan Your Waste Strategy

Decide how you will handle every type of waste before you leave. This means trash bags for solid waste, a system for gray water, and supplies for human waste (trowel and toilet paper bags at minimum, WAG bags for desert or alpine environments).

### Download Maps and Regulations

Know the rules for the specific land you are visiting. Different BLM districts and national forests have different regulations about fires, camping distance from water, and stay limits. Download offline maps since you likely will not have cell service. Tools like [WildCamp](https://wildcamp.nickwontquit.com) let you scout spots and understand land boundaries before you go.

### Pack a Fire Kit

If campfires are allowed, bring a small shovel, a bucket or large water container for extinguishing, and waterproof matches. If fires are restricted, bring a camp stove and leave the firewood at home.

### Bring Gloves and Extra Trash Bags

Plan to pick up trash left by previous visitors. Bring work gloves and a few extra bags so you can leave the site cleaner than you found it.

## Choosing Your Campsite

### Use Existing Sites

This is the single most important rule of dispersed camping. Always camp on ground that has already been disturbed by previous campers. Look for flattened areas, existing fire rings, and obvious vehicle pulloffs. Creating a new campsite by clearing brush, moving rocks, or flattening vegetation causes lasting damage.

### Stay 200 Feet From Water

Camp at least 200 feet (about 70 adult steps) from any stream, river, lake, or spring. This protects water quality, preserves riparian vegetation, and keeps wildlife corridors open. Animals depend on water sources and avoid them when humans camp too close.

### Avoid Fragile Ground

Cryptobiotic soil crusts in the desert, alpine tundra above treeline, and wet meadows are all extremely fragile ecosystems. These surfaces can take decades or centuries to recover from a single night of camping. Stick to bare ground, rock, gravel, or dry grass.

### Consider Your Impact on Others

If other campers are nearby, give them space. The whole point of dispersed camping is solitude. Do not set up 50 feet from an occupied site when there are other options available.

## The Campsite Checklist

Use this checklist every time you set up and break down camp. Print it, save it on your phone, or memorize it.

### Setting Up

- Camp only on previously disturbed ground
- Park your vehicle on an existing pulloff — do not drive off-road
- Set up your tent on bare ground, not on vegetation
- If there is an existing fire ring, use it — do not build a new one
- Identify your bathroom area (200 feet from water, camp, and trails)
- Store food securely (bear canister, vehicle, or hung from a tree depending on the area)

### During Your Stay

- Keep all trash contained — wind scatters loose items quickly
- Dispose of gray water (dishwater, rinse water) by straining food particles and scattering the water over a wide area at least 200 feet from any water source
- Use biodegradable soap sparingly and never wash directly in a stream or lake
- Keep campfires small and within an existing fire ring
- Burn only small sticks and branches that can be broken by hand — do not cut live trees
- Never leave a fire unattended, even for a short hike
- Keep noise to a minimum, especially after dark

### Managing Human Waste

- Dig catholes 6 to 8 inches deep and at least 200 feet from water, camp, and trails
- Pack out toilet paper in a sealed bag — buried toilet paper resurfaces and decomposes slowly, especially in dry climates
- In desert, alpine, or high-use areas, use WAG bags and pack out all waste
- Use hand sanitizer after any bathroom trip

### Breaking Camp

- Walk every square foot of your campsite and pick up all trash, including micro-trash (twist ties, food crumbs, small plastic bits)
- If you had a campfire, make sure it is completely extinguished: drown with water, stir the ashes, drown again, and feel with your hand — it should be cold to the touch
- Scatter any unused firewood so the site looks natural
- If you moved rocks or branches, put them back
- Take a final look from 20 feet away — your campsite should look like no one was there

## The Campfire Deep Dive

Campfires cause more damage on dispersed camping land than almost anything else. Here is how to do them right.

### Before Lighting a Fire

Check fire restrictions for your specific area. Restrictions change throughout the season and can be implemented with short notice during dry spells. A campfire during a fire ban can result in a fine of several hundred dollars and, more importantly, can start a wildfire that destroys thousands of acres.

### Building the Fire

Use an existing fire ring. If there is not one and fires are allowed, build a mound fire on mineral soil or use a fire pan that catches all ash. Keep the fire small — you should be able to extinguish it with a single pot of water at any moment.

### Extinguishing the Fire

This is where most people fail. A fire is not out just because the flames are gone. Hot coals can reignite hours later when wind picks up.

Pour water on the fire. Stir the ashes and coals. Pour more water. Stir again. Feel the ashes with the back of your hand. If they are warm, repeat the process. The ashes must be cold to the touch before you walk away. Every single time.

## Beyond the Basics

### Pick Up After Others

Carry out trash left by previous campers. It takes five minutes and makes an enormous difference. This is how you leave a site better than you found it.

### Minimize Campsite Sprawl

Keep your camp footprint small. The more area you trample, the more vegetation you damage. Set up your kitchen, tent, and living area in a compact arrangement on already-bare ground.

### Respect Wildlife

Observe animals from a distance. Do not feed wildlife — ever. A squirrel that learns to associate humans with food becomes a nuisance, and a bear that does the same becomes a dead bear. Store food, trash, and scented items (toothpaste, sunscreen, lip balm) in animal-proof containers or your vehicle.

### Respect Cultural Sites

If you encounter arrowheads, pottery shards, rock art, or ruins, do not touch or remove them. These sites are protected by federal law, and disturbing them carries serious penalties. Take photos, leave everything in place, and report significant finds to the local land management office.

## The Bottom Line

Dispersed camping is a privilege, not a right. Public land managers can and do close areas when overuse and abuse make it necessary. Every time you leave a clean campsite, you are voting to keep that land open for the next person.

The checklist is simple: use existing sites, manage your waste, respect fire restrictions, and leave no trace of your visit. Do these things consistently and you are doing your part to keep America's public lands open and wild.
