## What Is BLM Land?

The Bureau of Land Management (BLM) manages roughly 245 million acres of public land across the United States, most of it in the western states. This land belongs to the American public, and one of its most popular uses is free dispersed camping — also called boondocking.

Unlike national parks or developed campgrounds, BLM land rarely has facilities. There are no picnic tables, no fire rings, no running water, and no camp hosts. What you get instead is freedom: the ability to camp almost anywhere on public land, for free, with nothing between you and the landscape.

For many campers, van lifers, and overlanders, BLM land is the backbone of affordable outdoor travel. But finding it and understanding the rules can be confusing if you are just getting started.

## Step 1: Understand Where BLM Land Exists

BLM land is concentrated in 12 western states: Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Utah, Washington, and Wyoming. Nevada alone has over 48 million acres of BLM land, covering nearly 67 percent of the state.

If you are camping east of the Mississippi, BLM land is virtually nonexistent. In that case, national forests and state forests are your best options for dispersed camping.

## Step 2: Use Maps to Locate BLM Parcels

Finding BLM land used to require paper maps from local field offices. Today, several digital tools make it much easier.

### Official BLM Maps

The BLM's own Navigator map (blm.gov) shows land ownership boundaries. It works, but the interface can be clunky and slow, especially on mobile devices.

### WildCamp

[WildCamp](https://wildcamp.nickwontquit.com) overlays public land boundaries directly on an interactive map, making it easy to see BLM land, national forest land, and known dispersed camping spots all in one view. It is free to use and designed specifically for dispersed campers who want to find spots quickly without juggling multiple apps.

### Other Tools

Apps like Gaia GPS and onX Offroad also show land ownership layers, though most require paid subscriptions for full access. FreeRoam and iOverlander have user-submitted camp spots, but they do not always show land boundaries.

## Step 3: Check for Restrictions

Not all BLM land allows camping. Some areas are closed to overnight use for resource protection, wildlife management, or other reasons. Here is how to verify:

### Look for Signs

When you arrive at BLM land, look for kiosk signs or posted notices. These will indicate any camping restrictions, fire bans, or seasonal closures.

### Contact the Local Field Office

Each BLM district has a field office staffed with people who can tell you exactly where camping is and is not allowed. A quick phone call before your trip can save you a lot of hassle.

### Check for Special Designations

Wilderness Study Areas (WSAs), Areas of Critical Environmental Concern (ACECs), and other special designations may have additional rules. Some prohibit vehicles beyond certain points, while others restrict camping to designated sites.

## Step 4: Know the General Rules

BLM dispersed camping follows a fairly consistent set of rules across the West, though local variations exist.

### Stay Limit

You can camp in one spot for up to 14 days within any 28-day period. After 14 days, you must move at least 25 miles before camping again on BLM land in that district.

### Camp on Existing Sites

Always use previously disturbed areas for camping. Look for existing fire rings, flattened ground, or obvious pulloffs. Creating new campsites by clearing vegetation or leveling ground is not allowed and damages the landscape.

### Vehicles Stay on Roads

Drive only on established roads and trails. Off-road driving on BLM land is illegal in most areas and can result in fines. Park your vehicle on an existing pulloff or cleared area adjacent to the road.

### Pack It Out

There are no trash cans on BLM land. Everything you bring in must leave with you. This includes food waste, toilet paper, and gray water. Many experienced dispersed campers carry a dedicated trash bag and a portable waste system.

### Human Waste

If there are no vault toilets nearby, you must bury human waste in a cathole at least 6 to 8 inches deep and 200 feet from any water source, trail, or camp. In some desert areas where decomposition is extremely slow, pack-out systems like WAG bags are strongly recommended.

### Campfires

Campfires are generally allowed on BLM land where there is no fire restriction in effect. Use an existing fire ring if one exists, keep fires small, and make sure they are completely extinguished before leaving. During dry seasons, fire restrictions are common and can carry steep fines if violated.

## Step 5: Find the Best Spots

Knowing where BLM land is located is step one. Finding a great campsite on that land is step two. Here are some strategies.

### Follow Dirt Roads

The best dispersed sites are almost always down dirt roads, away from highways. A vehicle with decent ground clearance opens up exponentially more options. Look for side roads branching off county or forest roads — these often lead to established camping areas.

### Look for Water Features

Campsites near rivers, lakes, or streams tend to be more established and popular. They also tend to fill up faster on weekends. If you want solitude, camp farther from water.

### Arrive Early

Popular BLM camping areas near national parks and recreation areas can fill up by mid-afternoon on weekends, especially during spring and fall. Arrive before noon for the best selection.

### Scout on Satellite View

Before you drive out, use satellite imagery to spot potential campsites. Look for clearings, pulloffs, and existing fire rings visible from above. This can save significant time compared to driving every side road in person.

### Use WildCamp to Plan Ahead

[WildCamp](https://wildcamp.nickwontquit.com) shows you where other dispersed campers have found spots on BLM land. Instead of guessing which dirt road to turn down, you can plan your route around known good locations. This is especially useful when you are exploring a new area for the first time.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Camping too close to the road.** It seems convenient, but headlights from passing vehicles will wake you up all night. Pull back at least 100 feet from the main road if possible.

**Not bringing enough water.** BLM land in the desert is dry. Running out of water in a remote area is a serious safety issue. Carry at least one gallon per person per day, plus reserves.

**Ignoring fire restrictions.** A campfire violation on BLM land can result in a fine of $500 or more. Always check current restrictions before lighting a fire.

**Leaving without checking your site.** Walk your entire campsite before leaving. Pick up any micro-trash — small bits of food wrapper, bottle caps, or cigarette butts left by previous campers. Leave it better than you found it.

## The Bottom Line

BLM land is one of the greatest resources available to American campers. Millions of acres of free, public land are waiting for you — no reservation required. With the right preparation and respect for the land, dispersed camping on BLM land can become your favorite way to experience the outdoors.
