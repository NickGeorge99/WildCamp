## What Is Dispersed Camping?

Dispersed camping means camping outside of a developed campground on public land. There are no reserved sites, no hookups, no picnic tables, and usually no bathrooms. You find a spot, set up camp, and take care of yourself.

It goes by several names. Some people call it boondocking, others call it wild camping or primitive camping. Whatever you call it, the concept is the same: free camping on public land with minimal facilities and maximum freedom.

If you have only ever camped in developed campgrounds with bathrooms and fire rings, dispersed camping might sound intimidating. But with a little preparation, it is one of the most rewarding ways to experience the outdoors. This guide covers everything a beginner needs to know.

## Where Can You Camp for Free?

Dispersed camping is allowed on most Bureau of Land Management (BLM) land and national forest land in the United States. Together, these agencies manage hundreds of millions of acres, primarily in the western states.

### BLM Land

The BLM manages about 245 million acres. Most of this land is open to dispersed camping unless specifically posted otherwise. BLM land is most abundant in Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, Oregon, Idaho, and Montana.

### National Forests

The U.S. Forest Service manages 193 million acres of national forest land. Most national forests allow dispersed camping, though some have restrictions near trailheads, developed areas, or water sources. Each national forest publishes its own rules, so check with the local ranger district before heading out.

### Where You Cannot Camp

Dispersed camping is generally not allowed in national parks, state parks, national monuments managed by the National Park Service, or on private land. Some national monuments managed by the BLM or Forest Service do allow it — the distinction depends on the managing agency.

## How to Find a Spot

Finding your first dispersed campsite can feel overwhelming. Here is a simple process that works.

### Start With a Map

Use a tool like [WildCamp](https://wildcamp.nickwontquit.com) to identify public land near your destination. WildCamp shows BLM and national forest boundaries along with known dispersed camping spots, so you can plan before you leave home.

### Look for Dirt Roads

Most dispersed campsites are along dirt roads that branch off paved highways or main forest roads. Look for side roads with pulloffs or clearings. Previously used sites will have flattened ground and often an existing fire ring.

### Use Satellite Imagery

Before driving to an area, zoom in on satellite view in your mapping tool. You can often spot clearings, pulloffs, and flat areas that make good campsites. This saves time compared to driving every road blindly.

### Follow the Signs

On BLM and national forest land, look for road markers and kiosk signs. These will tell you if camping is restricted in a particular area. If there are no signs prohibiting camping and you are on public land, you are most likely good to set up camp.

## What to Bring

Dispersed camping requires more self-sufficiency than campground camping. Here is what to pack.

### Water

This is the most important item on the list. Most dispersed sites have no water access. Bring at least one gallon of water per person per day. For longer trips, consider bringing a larger container or portable water filtration system if you will be near a stream or lake.

### Shelter

A tent, hammock, vehicle, or van — whatever you sleep in, make sure it is appropriate for the conditions. Check weather forecasts carefully. Desert nights can drop below freezing even when daytime temperatures are in the 80s.

### Food and Cooking

Bring a portable camp stove and fuel. Do not rely on being able to have a campfire — fire restrictions are common during dry seasons. Store food securely, especially in bear country. A hard-sided cooler and bear canister are smart investments.

### Waste Management

Pack trash bags for all your waste. Bring a small shovel or trowel for digging catholes (for human waste). WAG bags are required in some areas and a good idea everywhere. Bring hand sanitizer and biodegradable soap.

### Navigation

Cell service is unreliable or nonexistent at most dispersed camping spots. Download offline maps before you leave. A dedicated GPS device is a worthwhile backup for remote areas.

### Recovery Gear

If you are driving on dirt roads, carry basic recovery gear: a shovel, traction boards or a traction mat, a tow strap, and a tire repair kit. Getting stuck on a remote BLM road with no cell service is a bad situation you want to avoid.

### First Aid

Carry a well-stocked first aid kit. Include items for blisters, cuts, burns, insect stings, and any personal medications. The nearest hospital may be hours away.

## Campsite Selection

When you arrive at a potential site, evaluate it before setting up.

### Check the Ground

Look for flat ground free of rocks and roots. Avoid dry washes or creek beds — these can flood suddenly during rainstorms, even when the sky above you is clear.

### Consider Wind

Exposed ridgelines and open desert flats can have brutal wind. If wind is forecasted, look for a site with natural wind protection — trees, rock formations, or a slight depression in the terrain.

### Distance From Roads

Camp far enough from the main road that passing vehicles will not disturb you. A hundred feet is usually enough, but more distance means more privacy and quiet.

### Proximity to Water

Camp at least 200 feet from any water source (streams, rivers, lakes, springs). This protects water quality and wildlife that depends on these water sources.

## Safety Tips for New Dispersed Campers

### Tell Someone Your Plans

Always let someone know where you are going and when you expect to return. Share your GPS coordinates or a pin drop when you arrive at your site if you have service.

### Know Your Limits

Start with an area that is relatively accessible. Do not attempt a remote four-wheel-drive road on your first outing. Build experience gradually.

### Wildlife Awareness

Learn about the wildlife in your camping area. In bear country, store food in a bear canister or hang it from a tree. In snake country, watch where you step and where you put your hands. In moose country, give these animals a wide berth — they are more dangerous than they look.

### Weather

Weather in the backcountry changes fast. Thunderstorms can appear quickly in the mountains, and flash floods are a real risk in desert canyons. Check forecasts before you leave and monitor conditions throughout your stay.

### Fire Safety

If campfires are allowed, keep them small and manageable. Use an existing fire ring if one is present. Never leave a fire unattended. Before you leave, drown the fire with water, stir the ashes, and feel them with your hand to make sure they are cold. One escaped campfire can burn thousands of acres.

## The 14-Day Rule

On both BLM and national forest land, you can generally stay in one spot for up to 14 days. After that, you must move — typically at least 25 miles on BLM land, or to a different area on Forest Service land. This rule prevents long-term camping and keeps sites available for other visitors.

## Start Simple

Your first dispersed camping trip does not need to be deep in the backcountry. Find a spot on public land within a couple hours of home. Spend one night. Figure out what you forgot, what you did not need, and what you wish you had brought. Each trip gets easier.

Tools like [WildCamp](https://wildcamp.nickwontquit.com) make finding that first spot much less stressful. Browse the map, find a spot on public land near you, and go. The hardest part is the first time — after that, you will wonder why you ever paid for a campsite.

## Final Thoughts

Dispersed camping opens up a world of free, uncrowded outdoor experiences on America's public lands. It requires more preparation than pulling into a developed campground, but the payoff is worth it: solitude, stunning scenery, and the satisfaction of being truly self-sufficient in the outdoors. Start simple, respect the land, and you will never look at camping the same way again.
