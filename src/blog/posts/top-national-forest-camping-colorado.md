## Why Colorado's National Forests Are Perfect for Dispersed Camping

Colorado is home to 11 national forests covering over 14 million acres of mountain terrain. That is more national forest land than almost any other state, and the vast majority of it is open to free dispersed camping.

What makes Colorado special is the variety. You can camp in alpine meadows above 10,000 feet, deep pine forests along rushing rivers, or open parks with views of 14,000-foot peaks. The state's extensive network of forest roads means there are thousands of established dispersed sites waiting for anyone willing to leave the pavement behind.

If you are tired of crowded campgrounds and expensive reservation systems, Colorado's national forests are where you want to be.

## Best National Forests for Dispersed Camping

### White River National Forest

White River is the most visited national forest in the country, thanks to ski resorts like Vail and Aspen. But venture past the resort towns and you will find excellent dispersed camping with far fewer people.

The areas around Sylvan Lake, Yeoman Park, and the Fryingpan River corridor offer established dispersed sites in beautiful mountain settings. Forest Road 400 and its side roads are popular with campers looking for riverside spots. Elevation ranges from 7,000 to over 12,000 feet, so summer temperatures are comfortable even when the Front Range is baking.

Be aware that some popular areas near the ski towns restrict camping to designated sites or require permits during peak season. Check with the local ranger district before heading out.

### San Juan National Forest

The San Juan Mountains in southwestern Colorado are some of the most dramatic peaks in the Rockies, and the San Juan National Forest provides incredible dispersed camping access. The area around Silverton, Ouray, and Telluride is famous for alpine scenery.

Forest roads in the San Juan give access to high-altitude camping near old mining ruins, alpine lakes, and passes above 12,000 feet. Many of these roads require high-clearance or four-wheel-drive vehicles, but the rewards are enormous. Spots along the Alpine Loop and South Mineral Creek are particularly stunning.

The season is short at these elevations. Most high-country roads do not open until late June or July and close again with the first heavy snows in September or October.

### Pike National Forest

Just an hour from Denver and Colorado Springs, Pike National Forest is the most accessible national forest for Front Range residents. Despite its proximity to two major cities, there is plenty of room to spread out.

The Lost Creek Wilderness area draws hikers, but the surrounding forest roads offer numerous dispersed camping opportunities. The areas around Tarryall Creek, Goose Creek, and the Rampart Range are popular choices. Many sites are accessible with a standard vehicle, making Pike a great option for beginners.

Because of its proximity to the metro area, Pike can get crowded on summer weekends, especially along the Rampart Range Road. Weekdays or shoulder seasons are much quieter.

### Gunnison National Forest

Gunnison National Forest in central Colorado is an underrated gem for dispersed camping. The forest surrounds the town of Gunnison and extends into the West Elk, Raggeds, and Fossil Ridge wilderness areas.

The Taylor River corridor and Taylor Park area are dispersed camping hotspots with riverside sites and mountain views. Cottonwood Pass, before it reaches the pavement on the eastern side, has excellent high-altitude camping with views of the Collegiate Peaks.

Gunnison is less crowded than forests closer to Denver, and the landscape mixes dense aspen groves with open sagebrush parks and alpine terrain. Fall camping here during peak aspen color in late September is unforgettable.

### Rio Grande National Forest

Rio Grande National Forest in the San Luis Valley is one of Colorado's least visited forests, which makes it one of the best for solitude. The forest encompasses the headwaters of the Rio Grande and some of the most remote terrain in the state.

Dispersed camping along the roads near Creede, South Fork, and the La Garita Wilderness offers a mix of river canyons, high mountain parks, and dense spruce forests. The Continental Divide runs through the forest, and camping near the divide at elevations above 11,000 feet delivers some of the most expansive views in Colorado.

Wildlife is abundant here. Elk, mule deer, black bears, and mountain lions all call this forest home. Practice proper food storage and be aware of your surroundings.

## General Rules for Dispersed Camping in Colorado National Forests

Each national forest sets its own specific rules, but the following general guidelines apply across most of Colorado.

### Stay Limit

You can camp in one spot for up to 14 days. After that, you must move to a new location, typically at least 10 miles away.

### Distance Restrictions

Camp at least 100 feet from any road, trail, stream, or lake unless you are at an established site that is closer. Some forests have specific buffer distances, so check local regulations.

### Fire Rules

Campfires are allowed in most national forests when there is no fire restriction in effect. Use an existing fire ring if available. During dry summers, Stage 1 or Stage 2 fire restrictions are common and may prohibit all open flames, including some camp stoves. Always check current restrictions before your trip at the forest's website or by calling the ranger district.

### Motor Vehicles

Vehicles must stay on designated roads and trails. Do not drive off-road to create a new campsite. Park on established pulloffs adjacent to forest roads.

## Tips for Finding the Best Sites

### Explore Side Roads

The best dispersed campsites are rarely on the main forest road. Turn down side roads and spur roads — the farther you go, the fewer people you will encounter. A forest road map or a tool like [WildCamp](https://wildcamp.nickwontquit.com) can help you identify which side roads lead to camping areas.

### Check Elevation and Season

Colorado's camping season is heavily dependent on elevation. Low-elevation sites (6,000 to 8,000 feet) open in April or May, while high-altitude sites above 10,000 feet may not be accessible until July. Snow can return as early as September at the highest elevations.

### Watch for Afternoon Thunderstorms

From late June through August, afternoon thunderstorms are a near-daily occurrence in Colorado's mountains. They typically build between 1 and 4 PM. Avoid exposed ridgelines and be prepared for heavy rain, hail, and lightning. Set up camp in a sheltered location below treeline when possible.

### Water Sources

Many dispersed sites in Colorado are near streams and rivers, making water access easier than in the desert states. However, always filter or treat water from natural sources. Giardia and other waterborne pathogens are present in Colorado's streams.
